Aircraft-made snow: an automated census from open radar and flight data

Airliners climbing and descending through supercooled cloud freeze some of its droplets, and the ice falls out as thin streaks of snow on weather radar. This project finds those streaks automatically, matches each to the aircraft that made it, and counts how often it happens.

    The aircraft

    Sixteen flights matched to streaks, about twelve more than chance

    Most were airliners on the arrival pattern, and most crossed the cloud at about 3 km, in air at −16 to −18 °C; their snow showed up on the radar 15 to 50 minutes later. Rotated copies of the day's flights produce about four such matches, so a few of these are probably chance; which ones cannot be told apart.

    Altitude is pressure altitude. Fall speed is the height the ice had to fall to reach the radar beam divided by the delay; snow crystals and flakes fall at 0.3–1.5 m/s. Snowfall is water equivalent from the reflectivity, uncertain by a factor of a few. Two rows look physically odd (an airliner seeding at 20 000 ft; one only 500 m above the beam) and are likely among the chance matches.

    The test

    How we know it was the aircraft

    A thin line of snow near a busy airport will often lie along some flight path by coincidence. So every comparison is repeated with all of the day's flights rotated around the radar to other bearings: same shapes, same timing, same wind, wrong place. Rotated flights match streaks too, and that is the chance level.

    A match counts only if the streak lies along the flight's own track carried by the wind, the air there was clear before the aircraft arrived, the air was below −4 °C, and the ice would have had to fall at the speed snow falls to reach the radar beam when it did.

    What this does not prove on its own: rotating flights also breaks any link between airport corridors and echo that lines up with them for other reasons. An early version of the test found "matches" on warm July days, when no aircraft can make snow: insects along convergence lines and ground clutter near the approach paths. Radar can tell such echo from precipitation by how uniform its returns are (the correlation coefficient); with it masked, those days came out clean. How often false alarms remain is measured below, on warm days at every radar in the census.
